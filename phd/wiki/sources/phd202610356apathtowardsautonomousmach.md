# 10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach

Original: [[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf]]

## Overview

This is Yann LeCun's 2022 position paper "A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence" (Meta/FAIR), proposing a comprehensive cognitive architecture and research roadmap for building autonomous intelligent agents that learn like humans and animals — largely through observation, with minimal trial-and-error interaction. 

The paper's central diagnosis is that current [[deep-learning]] and [[reinforcement-learning]] systems are catastrophically **sample-inefficient compared to biological learners** because they lack [[world-models]] — internal, predictive models of how the world works that support common sense, reasoning, and planning.

## The Cognitive Architecture

LeCun proposes a modular, fully differentiable architecture composed of:
- a **[[Perception Module]]** estimating world state from sensors (possibly hierarchically);
- a **[[world-models]]** that fills in missing information and predicts plausible future states, handling uncertainty via latent variables;
- a **[[Cost module]]** split into an immutable **[[Intrinsic Cost module]]** (hard-wired drives: pain, hunger, curiosity, social/empathic drives — analogous to the amygdala) and a **[[Trainable Critic module]]** (predicts future intrinsic energy, trained from a **[[Short-Term Memory Module]]** of state/energy triplets, similar to A2C critic training);
- an **[[Actor Module]]** that proposes and optimizes action sequences to minimize predicted cost, operating in two modes: **[[Mode-1 perception-action episode]]** (fast, reactive, System-1-like) and **[[Mode-2 perception-action episode]]** (deliberate model-predictive control / System-2-like, akin to [[model-predictive-control]]);
- a **[[configurator-module]]**, the executive-control component that dynamically modulates all other modules' parameters and attention for the task at hand — explicitly flagged as the least understood, most "mysterious" part of the proposal, with no specified mechanism for how it learns to decompose tasks into subgoals.

The architecture is explicitly mapped onto mammalian brain structures (prefrontal cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, basal ganglia, pre-motor cortex) and onto **Kahneman's System1/System2 dichotomy**.

## JEPA and Non-Contrastive Self-Supervised Learning

The paper's core technical contribution is the **[[joint-embedding-predictive-architecture]]** (JEPA), a non-generative architecture that predicts the *representation* of a future/missing input rather than the input itself, sidestepping the impossibility of pixel-level prediction under uncertainty. 

LeCun frames this within a broader **[[energy-based-models-ebm]]** formalism, distinguishing **[[Contrastive methods]]** (which push down energy on real data and up on negative samples, but suffer the curse of dimensionality) from **[[Regularized Methods]]** (which minimize the volume of low-energy space directly, argued to be more promising long-term). **[[VICReg]]** and [[Barlow Twins]] are highlighted as non-contrastive training criteria; VICReg is discussed in detail as a "dimension-contrastive" method using variance and covariance losses to prevent representational collapse.

Extending JEPA hierarchically — **[[Hierarchical JEPA (H-JEPA)]]** — is proposed as the mechanism for multi-timescale, multi-abstraction prediction and hierarchical planning under uncertainty, where high-level "actions" become conditions to be satisfied by lower levels (an idea traced to classical control theory servomechanisms).

## Reasoning, Uncertainty, and Common Sense

Reasoning in this architecture is reframed as energy minimization/constraint satisfaction rather than symbolic manipulation, addressing the paper's opening tension between gradient-based learning and logic-based reasoning. Multiple types of [[Aleatoric Uncertainty]] and [[Epistemic Uncertainty]] are catalogued, handled via regularized latent variables sampled at planning time (with combinatorial explosion of trajectories requiring pruning/search methods like Monte-Carlo Tree Search).

The paper explicitly argues **against** two dominant contemporary positions: that scaling large transformer/language models alone yields human-level intelligence, and that "reward is enough" via model-free RL. Both are called sample-inefficient or representationally limited (tokenized, generative models struggle with continuous high-dimensional uncertainty; no dynamically specifiable goals). Large language models are said to possess only "shallow" common sense disconnected from physical reality, since they never interact with the world. [[Common Sense]] is instead characterized as the product of a configurable world-model engine capturing self-consistency of observations.

## Open Problems

LeCun candidly lists unresolved issues: whether H-JEPA can actually be built and trained from video and learn abstract concept hierarchies; which latent-regularization mechanism (discrete, low-dim, sparse, stochastic) works best; how the actor should search discrete/non-smooth action spaces; how short-term memory should represent beliefs; and above all how the configurator learns to decompose tasks into subgoals.

## Related work referenced

The paper situates itself against [[Dyna Architecture]] (Sutton's Mode-2-like model-based RL), the Director hierarchical world-model system, [[Model-Predictive Control]], and prior joint-embedding work (Siamese networks, [[PIRL]], [[SimCLR]], BYOL, MoCo).

## Key claims

- Current AI and machine learning systems require vastly more training trials than humans and animals to achieve reliable performance, even in domains where humans excel with minimal practice. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 1–6]]) — "to be reliable, current ML systems need to be trained with very large numbers of trials so that even the rarest combination of situations will be encountered frequently during training"
- Humans and animals learn world models—internal models of how the world works—which enable their superior learning efficiency and ability to reason and plan. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 1–6]]) — "The answer may lie in the ability of humans and many animals to learn world models, internal models of how the world works."
- A key challenge for AI is enabling machines to learn representations of the world, make predictions, and act through observation rather than requiring expensive interactions. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 1–6]]) — "How can machines learn to represent the world, learn to predict, and learn to act largely by observation?"
- Current gradient-based learning approaches are difficult to reconcile with logic-based symbolic reasoning, presenting a major challenge for machine reasoning and planning. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 1–6]]) — "How can machine reason and plan in ways that are compatible with gradient-based learning? Our best approaches to learning rely on estimating and using the gradient of a loss, which can only be performed with differentiable architectures and is difficult to reconcile with logic-based symbolic reasoning."
- Machines need to learn hierarchical representations at multiple levels of abstraction and time scales, decomposing complex actions into sequences of lower-level ones. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 1–6]]) — "How can machines learn to represent percepts and action plans in a hierarchical manner, at multiple levels of abstraction, and multiple time scales?"
- Common sense in animals and humans can be understood as a collection of world models that enable agents to predict what is likely, plausible, or impossible. The system is a scalar-valued function F (x, y) that produces low energy values when x and y are compatible and higher values when they are not."
- EBM formulation enables representation of multi-modal dependencies where multiple values of y can be compatible with a given x. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 13–18]]) — "The EBM implicit function formulation enables the system to represent multi-modal dependencies in which multiple values of y are compatible with a given x. The set of y compatible with a given x may be a single point, multiple discrete points, a manifold, or a collection of points and manifolds."
- A predictive world model should learn representations such that future representations are maximally informative and easily predictable from past representations. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 13–18]]) — "The general learning principle is as follows: given two inputs x and y, learn two functions that compute representations sx = gx(x) and sy = gy(y) such that (1) sx and sy are maximally informative about x and y and (2) sy can easily be predicted from sx."
- Through hierarchical prediction at increasingly abstract levels and time scales, complex world concepts may be learned including edges, depth, occlusion, object permanence, and intuitive physics. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 13–18]]) — "One may imagine that through predictions at increasingly abstract levels of representation and increasingly long time scales, more and more complex concepts about how the world works may be acquired in a hierarchical fashion."
- Latent variables in energy-based models can parameterize the set of possible relationships between an input x and compatible outputs y, representing information about y that cannot be extracted from x. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 19–21]]) — "The latent variable can be seen as parameterizing the set of possible relationships between an x and a set of compatible y. Latent variables represent information about y that cannot be extracted from x."
- Inference in latent-variable EBMs consists of finding the latent variable value that minimizes the energy function. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 19–21]]) — "Inference consists in finding the latent that minimizes the energy ˇz = argminz∈Z Ew(x, y, z)."
- After latent variable inference by minimization, the latent variable can be eliminated from the energy function to yield a simplified energy function Fw(x,y). ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 19–21]]) — "Fw(x, y) = min z∈Z Ew(x, y, z) = Ew(x, y, ˇz)"
- Energy-based models do not necessarily require probabilistic interpretation, though many can be converted to probabilistic models. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 19–21]]) — "Although many EBMs can easily be turned into probabilistic models, e.g. through a Gibbs distribution, this is not at all a necessity."
- Training an EBM requires designing a loss functional that makes the energy of training samples lower than the energies of non-matching alternatives. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 19–21]]) — "Training an EBM comes down to devising a suitable loss functional L(x, y, Fw(x, y)), which can be expressed directly as a function of the parameter vector L(x, y, w), and such that minimizing this loss will make the energy of the training sample Fw(x, y) lower than the energies Fw(x, ˆy) of any ˆy different from y."
- Without explicit provisions to ensure higher energies for non-matching outputs, the energy landscape can collapse into a flat surface giving essentially the same energy to all output values. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 19–21]]) — "Without a specific provision to ensure that Fw(x, y′) > Fw(x, y) whenever ˆy ≠ y the energy landscape may suffer a collapse: given an x the energy landscape could become "flat", giving essentially the same energy to all values of y."
- Deterministic predictive architectures cannot suffer from energy collapse because they produce only a single output. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 19–21]]) — "A regular predictive or deterministic-generative architecture (Figure 10(a)) cannot collapse. For any x, a single ỹ is produced."
- Generative latent-variable architectures can collapse when the latent variable has excessive information capacity relative to the set of compatible outputs. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 19–21]]) — "A generative latent-variable architecture (non-deterministic generative) (Figure 10(b)) can collapse when the latent variable has too much information capacity."
- Auto-encoder architectures can collapse when their representation has sufficient information capacity to learn the identity function or reconstruct regions larger than the high-density data region. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 19–21]]) — "An auto-encoder (AE) (Figure 10(c)) can collapse when the representation sy has too much information capacity. For example, if the dimension of sy is equal or higher than that of y, the AE could learn the identity function."
- Joint embedding architectures can collapse when encoders produce insufficient or constant and equal representations. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 19–21]]) — "A Joint Embedding Architecture (JEA) (Figure 10(d)) can collapse when the information carried by sx and/or sy are insufficient. If the encoders ignore the inputs, and produce constant and equal codes sx = sy, the entire space will have zero energy."
- Two main approaches exist for designing loss functions to prevent energy collapse: contrastive methods and regularized methods. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 19–21]]) — "There are two approaches: contrastive methods and regularized methods."
- Contrastive methods for EBM training push down energy on training samples and pull up energy on contrastive samples, but only affect the energy surface where contrastive samples are placed. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Contrastive methods (top right) push down on the energy of training samples (blue dots) and pulls up on the energies of suitably-placed contrastive samples (green dots)."
- Regularized methods minimize the volume of low-energy regions while pushing down energy on training samples, causing low-energy regions to wrap around data-dense areas. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "regularized methods (bottom right) push down on the energy of training samples and use a regularizer term that minimizes the volume of low-energy regions."
- Contrastive methods require the number of contrastive samples to grow exponentially with the dimensionality of y space in high dimensions. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "a disadvantage of contrastive methods is that the number of contrastive samples necessary to make an energy surface adopt a good shape may grow exponentially with the dimension of y space."
- Distance-dependent hinge loss makes energy grow at least quadratically with distance from the data manifold. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "This makes the energy grow at least quadratically with the distance to the data manifold."
- Contrastive methods include Siamese networks, DrLIM, PIRL, MoCO, SimCLR, CPT, and other recent approaches with pairs where x is a distorted version of y. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Contrastive methods are very popular, particularly for Siamese network architectures trained with pairs where x is a distorted or corrupted version of y and ˆy another random (or suitably chosen) training sample. This includes such methods as the original Siamese net, as well as more recent methods including DrLIM, PIRL, MoCO, SimCLR, CPT, and others."
- Generative Adversarial Networks can be seen as contrastive methods where contrastive samples are produced by a trainable generator network. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Generative Adversarial Networks can also be seen as contrastive methods in which the ˆy are produced by the trainable generator network."
- Denoising Auto-Encoders and Masked Auto-Encoders are examples of contrastive training where contrastive samples are generated by corrupting clean data. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Denoising Auto-Encoders and their special case, Masked Auto-Encoders, are also examples of contrastive training methods in which the ˆy is generated by corrupting the clean y."
- The main problems with contrastive methods are designing a scheme to generate suitable contrastive samples and the exponential growth requirement in high dimensions. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "First, one has to design a scheme to generate or pick suitable ˆy. Second, when y is in a high-dimensional space, and if the EBM is flexible, it may require a very large number of contrastive samples to ensure that the energy is higher in all dimensions unoccupied by the local data distribution."
- Regularized methods are more promising than contrastive methods because they avoid the curse of dimensionality by minimizing the volume of low-energy regions. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Regularized methods for EBM training are much more promising in the long run than contrastive methods because they can eschew the curse of dimensionality that plagues contrastive methods."
- Examples of non-contrastive regularized methods include sparse modeling, sparse auto-encoders, and VAE. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Examples include sparse modeling, sparse auto-encoders, and auto-encoders with noisy latent variables, such as VAE."
- Contrastive and regularized methods can be used simultaneously on the same model. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "It is important to note that contrastive and regularized methods are not incompatible with each other, and can be used simultaneously on the same model."
- In latent-variable generative architectures, restricting information capacity of z restricts the volume of y space that can take low energy. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "In the latent-variable generative architecture, restricting the information capacity of z will restrict the volume of y space that can take low energy."
- In auto-encoder architectures, restricting information capacity of sy restricts the volume of y space that can be reconstructed with low energy. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Similarly, in the auto-encoder architecture, restricting the information capacity of sy will restrict the volume of y space that can be reconstructed with low energy."
- In Joint Embedding Architecture, maximizing information that sx and sy contain about x and y minimizes the volume of y space that can take low energy. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Lastly, in the Joint Embedding Architecture, Maximizing the information that sx contains about x and sy contains about y will minimize the volume of y space that can take low energy."
- JEPA combines Joint Embedding Architecture and Latent-Variable Generative Architecture and is non-generative, predicting the representation of y rather than y itself. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "In the following, we will focus on an architecture for SSL the Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) which can seen as a combination of the Joint Embedding Architecture and the Latent-Variable Generative Architecture. The JEPA is non-generative in that it does not actually predict y, but predicts the representation of y, sy from that of x, sx."
- The energy in JEPA is the prediction error between the predicted representation and actual representation in representation space. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "The energy is simply the prediction error in representation space: Ew(x, y, z) = D(sy, Pred(sx, z))"
- JEPA's main advantage is that it performs predictions in representation space, avoiding the need to predict every detail of y. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "The main advantage of JEPA is that it performs predictions in representation space, eschewing the need to predict every detail of y."
- JEPA can represent multi-modality through encoder invariance properties that map multiple y values to the same sy. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "The encoder function sy = Enc(y) may have invariance properties. If all the y's in a set map to the same value of sy, all those y's will have identical energies."
- JEPA can represent multi-modality through a latent variable z that captures information necessary to predict sy not present in sx. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "The predictor may use a latent variable z to capture the information necessary to predict sy that is not present in sx."
- JEPA can be trained with non-contrastive methods using four criteria: maximize information in sx, maximize information in sy, make sy predictable from sx, and minimize information in z. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "The basic principle of such training is that (1) sx should be maximally informative about x; (2) sy should be maximally informative about y; (3) sy should be easily predictable from sx; and (4) z should have minimal information content."
- Criteria 1 and 2 in JEPA training prevent energy surface collapse by ensuring sx and sy carry maximum information about their inputs. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Criteria 1 and 2 prevent the energy surface from becoming flat by informational collapse. They ensure that sx and sy carry as much information as possible about their inputs."
- Criterion 3 in JEPA training is enforced by the energy term and ensures y is predictable from x in representation space. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Criterion 3 is enforced by the energy term D(sy, ˜sy) and ensures that y is predictable from x in representation space."
- Criterion 4 prevents informational collapse by forcing the model to predict sy with minimal help from the latent variable. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Criterion 4 prevents the system from falling victim to another type of informational collapse by forcing the model to predict sy with as little help from the latent as possible."
- If z has the same dimension as sy, the predictor could ignore sx and copy z to produce zero energy, creating a collapsed energy surface. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Imagine that z has the same dimension as sy. Assume that the predictor is a parameterized function (e.g. a neural net) that can choose to ignore sx and to simply copy z on its output ˜sy = z. For any sy it is possible to set ˇz = sy, which would make the energy D(sy, ˜sy) zero."
- Information content of latent variable z can be minimized by making it discrete, low-dimensional, sparse, or noisy. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "By making z discrete, low-dimensional, sparse, or noisy, among other methods."
- When z is discrete with K values, there can only be K possible values of sy with zero energy in a K-well energy landscape. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Hence, these can be the only values of sy with zero energy, and there are only K of them."
- When z is a low-dimensional vector, the set of possible predictions forms at most a d-dimensional manifold in representation space. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Then, assuming that Pred(sx, z) is a smooth function of z, the set of possible predictions will be at most a d-dimensional manifold in the space of sy."
- L1 regularization on z drives z to sparsity and causes low-energy regions to be approximated by unions of low-dimensional manifolds. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "This will drive ˇz to be sparse. As with classical sparse coding, this will cause the region of low energy to be approximated by a union of low-dimensional manifolds"
- Four classes of methods minimize information content of latent variables: discretization/quantification, dimensionality/rank minimization, sparsification, and fuzzyfication. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "we can mention four classes of methods: discretization/quantification (e.g. as in VQ-VAE (Walker et al., 2021), dimensionality/rank minimization (e.g. as in Implicit Rank-Minimizing AE (Jing et al., 2020), sparsification (as in linear sparse modeling (Olshausen and Field, 1996), LISTA (Gregor and LeCun, 2010b), and non-linear sparse modeling (Evtimova and LeCun, 2022)), and fuzzyfication (as in noisy AE (Doi et al., 2007), VAE (Kingma and Welling, 2013), and variants used in control problems (Henaff et al., 2019))."
- JEPA is preferable to generative models because predicting every pixel value in video frames is impossible, but JEPA can predict abstract representations. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "The ability of the JEPA to predict in representation space makes it considerably preferable to generative models that directly produce a prediction of y. In a video prediction scenario, it is essentially impossible to predict every pixel value of every future frame."
- VICReg and Barlow Twins are examples of non-contrastive criteria for JEPA training. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 22–27]]) — "Examples of such non-contrastive criteria for JEPA training include VICReg and Barlow Twins."
- VICReg is a non-sample-contrastive method that maximizes information content of representations by mapping them to higher-dimensional embeddings and driving the covariance matrix towards identity. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "VICReg is a non sample-contrastive method for training embeddings. The information content of the representations sx and sy is maximized by first mapping them to higher-dimensional embeddings vx and vy through an expander (e.g. a trainable neural net with a few layers). The loss function drives the covariance matrix of the embeddings towards the identity (e.g. computed over a batch)."
- VICReg can be characterized as dimension-contrastive rather than sample-contrastive. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "VICReg can be seen as a dimension-contrastive method as opposed to sample-contrastive methods."
- To maximize information content of a representation, the encoding function should be minimally surjective, meaning the volume of input sets mapping to the same representation should be minimal. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "sy is maximally informative about y if the function Encw(y) is minimally surjective, i.e. if the volume of sets of y that map to the same sy is minimal."
- VICReg uses two sub-criteria: components must not be constant, and components must be as independent of each other as possible. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "To maximize the information content of sx, VICReg uses the following two sub-criteria: (1) the components of sx must not be constant, (2) the components of sx must be as independent of each other as possible."
- VICReg employs a variance loss that maintains standard deviation of each component above a threshold over a batch. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "Variance: a hinge loss that maintains the standard deviation of each component of sy and vy above a threshold over a batch."
- VICReg uses a covariance loss that pushes covariance between different components towards zero, decorrelating the representation components. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "Covariance: a covariance loss in which the covariance between pairs of different components of vy are pushed towards zero. This has the effect of decorrelating the components of vy, which will in turn make the components of sy somewhat independent."
- VICReg's third criterion is the representation prediction error between the actual and predicted representations. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "The third criterion of VICReg is the representation prediction error D(sy, ˜sy)."
- In simple VICReg implementations, the predictor is the identity function, making representations invariant to transformations; more sophisticated versions use latent variables that may be discrete, low-dimensional, or stochastic. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "In the simplest implementations of VICReg, the predictor is constant (equal to the identity function), making the representations invariant to the transformation that turns x into y. In more sophisticated versions, the predictor may have no latent variable, or may depend on a latent variable that is either discrete, low dimensional, or stochastic."
- VICReg ensures different components of representations are different across a batch, contrasting over components rather than over vectors like traditional contrastive methods. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "While contrastive methods ensure that representations of different inputs in a batch are different, VICReg ensures that different components of representations over a batch are different. VICReg is contrastive over components, while traditional contrastive methods are contrastive over vectors, which requires a large number of contrastive samples."
- The most promising aspect of JEPA trained with VICReg is for learning hierarchical predictive world models. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "But the most promising aspect of JEPA trained with VICReg and similar non-contrastive methods is for learning hierarchical predictive world models, as we examine in the next section."
- JEPA architectures and predictor design create inductive biases that implicitly determine what information is predictable. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "What is predictable and what does not get represented is determined implicitly by the architectures of the encoders and predictor. They determine a inductive bias that defines what information is predictable or not."
- Prediction heads can be added to bias JEPA towards learning representations containing task-relevant information. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "But it would be useful to have a way to bias the system towards representations that contain information relevant to a class of tasks. This can be done by adding prediction heads that take ˜sy as input and are trained to predict variables that are easily derived from the data and known to be relevant to the task."
- JEPA models can learn abstract representations by eliminating irrelevant details, making the world more predictable. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "a JEPA can choose to train its encoders to eliminate irrelevant details of the inputs so as to make the representations more predictable. In other words, a JEPA will learn abstract representations that make the world predictable."
- Generative latent-variable models cannot eliminate irrelevant details the way JEPA can; they can only push such details into latent variables. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "It is important to note that generative latent-variable models are not capable of eliminating irrelevant details, other than by pushing them into a latent variable. This is because they do not produce abstract (and invariant) representations of y."
- Hierarchical JEPA enables multi-timescale and multi-level abstraction prediction, with lower levels for short-term detailed predictions and higher levels for long-term abstract predictions. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 28–30]]) — "The capacity of JEPA to learn abstractions suggests an extension of the architecture to handle prediction at multiple time scales and multiple levels of abstraction. Intuitively, low-level representations contain a lot of details about the input, and can be used to predict in the short term. But it may be difficult to produce accurate long-term predictions with the same level of details. Conversely high-level, abstract representation may enable long-term predictions, but at the cost of eliminating a lot of details."
- When driving a car, given a proposed sequence of steering and pedal actions over several seconds, drivers can accurately predict the car's trajectory over that same period. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "given a proposed sequence of actions on the steering wheel and pedals over the next several seconds, drivers can accurately predict the trajectory of their car over the same period"
- Trajectory details over longer driving periods are harder to predict because they depend on unpredictable external events like other cars, traffic lights, and pedestrians. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "The details of the trajectory over longer periods are harder to predict because they may depend on other cars, traffic lights, pedestrians, and other external events that are somewhat unpredictable"
- Drivers can make accurate higher-level predictions about arrival times even when detailed trajectories are unpredictable. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "the car will probably arrive at its destination within a predictable time frame"
- Discrete latent variables can represent multiple alternative routes in hierarchical prediction. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "A discrete latent variable may be used to represent multiple alternative routes"
- Hierarchical world state prediction with multiple levels can use convolutional and other modules with temporal pooling between levels. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "One can envision architectures of this type with many levels, possibly using convolutional and other modules, and using temporal pooling between levels to coarse-grain the representation and perform longer-term predictions"
- The ability to represent world state sequences at several levels of abstraction is essential to intelligent behavior. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "the ability to represent sequences of world states at several levels of abstraction is essential to intelligent behavior"
- Complex tasks can be decomposed into hierarchically structured subtasks at different levels of detail, instantiated when local conditions permit. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "With multi-level representations of world states and actions, a complex task can be decomposed into successively more detailed sub-tasks, instantiated into actions sequences when informed by local conditions"
- Hierarchical planning architectures can learn intermediate action representations rather than requiring them to be predefined. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "if one abides by the deep learning philosophy, those intermediate representations of action plans should also be learned"
- In hierarchical Mode-2 planning, high-level actions are actually targets for lower-level predicted states, functioning as conditions that lower-level states must satisfy. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "These high-level "actions" are not real actions but targets for the lower level predicted states. One can think of them as conditions that the lower-level state must satisfy in order for the high-level predictions to be accurate"
- Cost modules measure the extent to which a lower-level state satisfies a high-level condition to define subgoals. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "cost modules C(s[2]) and C(s[4]) take a lower-level state s[2] and a high-level condition a2[2] and measure to what extent the state satisfies the condition"
- Top-down greedy hierarchical planning can be improved by jointly optimizing action sequences across all layers. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "one may advantageously iterate the optimization so that high level and low-level action sequences are optimized jointly"
- The concept that an action is a condition to be satisfied by the level below originates from classical control theory. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "The idea that an action is merely a condition to be satisfied by the level below is actually an old one in control theory"
- A proportional servomechanism from classical control theory can be understood as receiving a target state with quadratic cost measuring distance to that target. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "a classical proportional servomechanism can be seen as being given a target state. A quadratic cost measures the squared distance between the target and the current state"
- Real-world uncertainty can arise from the world being intrinsically stochastic (aleatoric uncertainty type 1). ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "the world is intrinsically stochastic (aleatoric uncertainty, type 1)"
- Uncertainty can result from deterministic but chaotic worlds that are difficult to predict without infinitely precise perception (aleatoric uncertainty type 2). ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "the world is deterministic but chaotic, hence difficult to predict without infinitely precise perception (aleatoric uncertainty, type 2)"
- Partial observability of a deterministic world creates aleatoric uncertainty (type 3). ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "the world is deterministic but partially observable (aleatoric uncertainty type 3)"
- Epistemic uncertainty type 1 occurs when sensors provide only partial information about fully observable world states. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "the world is fully observable, but the sensors only give partial information about the world state (epistemic uncertainty, type 1)"
- Epistemic uncertainty type 2 occurs when the perception module's extracted representation lacks information necessary for accurate prediction. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "the representation of the world state extracted by the perception module does not contain the full information necessary for accurate prediction (epistemic uncertainty, type 2)"
- Epistemic uncertainty type 3 results from inaccurate world models due to limited representational power (bounded rationality). ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "the world model is inaccurate due to limitations of its representational power (bounded rationality or epistemic uncertainty, type 3)"
- Latent variables in predictors can represent prediction information that cannot be derived from prior observations. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "Uncertainty about predictions can be handled by predictors with latent variables. The latent variables contain information about the prediction that cannot be derived from the prior observation"
- Latent variables must be regularized to prevent energy collapse and force the system to predict without their help. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "The latent variables must be regularized to prevent an energy collapse and to force the system to predict as much as possible without the help of it"
- At planning time, latent variables are sampled from distributions created by applying Gibbs distributions to regularizers, producing different predictions. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "At planning time, latent variables are sampled from distributions obtained by applying a Gibbs distribution to the regularizers. Each sample leads to a different prediction"
- Regularizer parameters can be functions of previous states and retrieved memories to produce consistent latent sequences. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "the parameters of the regularizer can be functions of previous states and retrieved memories"
- The number of generated state trajectories grows exponentially when each latent variable has k discrete values and prediction progresses t time steps (k^t trajectories). ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "If each latent variable has k possible discrete values, the number of possible trajectories will grow as k^t, where t is the number of time steps"
- Directed search and pruning strategies are necessary to manage exponential growth in predicted trajectories. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "Directed search and pruning strategies must be employed"
- With multiple predicted trajectories, optimal action sequences can minimize average cost or combinations of average and variance to minimize risk. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 31–33]]) — "optimal action sequences can be computed that minimize the average cost, or a combination of average and variance of the cost so as to minimize risk"

- Stochasticity in predictions can be handled by pushing it into latent variables that may be optimized, predicted, or sampled rather than requiring models to represent distributions directly. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 34–39]]) — "we push the possible stochasticity of a predicted variable into a latent variable, which may be optimized, predicted, or sampled."

([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 34–39]]) — "if each latent variable has k possible discrete values, the number of possible trajectories will grow as k^t, where t is the number of time steps."

([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 43–48]]) — "If the actions and latent variables are continuous, and if the predictor and the cost modules are differentiable and relatively well behaved, one can use gradient-based methods to perform the search."
- High-level abstractions with discrete action spaces may require exhaustive or heuristic search methods like Monte-Carlo Tree Search instead of gradient-based methods. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 43–48]]) — "If the action space is discrete with low cardinality, the actor may use exhaustive search methods. If the action set cardinality, and hence the branching factor, are too large, the actor may have to resort to heuristic search methods, including Monte-Carlo Tree Search, or other gradient-free methods."
- World-model training should find hierarchical representations where planning becomes a continuous relaxation of an otherwise discrete problem. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 43–48]]) — "But the efficiency advantage of gradient-based search methods over gradient-free search methods motivates us to find ways for the world-model training procedure to find hierarchical representations with which the planning/reasoning problem constitutes a continuous relaxation of an otherwise discrete problem."
- It remains an open question whether the proposed type of reasoning can encompass all forms of reasoning that humans and animals are capable of. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 43–48]]) — "A remain question is whether the type of reasoning proposed here can encompass all forms of reasoning that humans and animals are capable of."
- Architectural diagrams in this paper use filled circles for observed variables or outputs of deterministic functions, and hollow circles for latent variables that must be inferred, minimized, varied, or sampled. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "Filled circles represent observed variables, or variables that are outputs of deterministic functions. Hollow circles represent latent variables, i.e. variables that must be inferred by minimizing some cost, varied over a set, or sampled from a distribution."
- Red rectangles in the diagrams represent energy terms that contribute additively to the total energy of the system with an implicit scalar output. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "Red rectangles represent energy terms. These modules have an implicit scalar output that contributes additively to the total energy of the system."
- Rounded rectangles represent deterministic functions with clear directionality from inputs to outputs, assumed to be differentiable and easily computable with unique outputs, often implemented as deep neural networks. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "Rounded rectangles represent deterministic functions, which may have one or several inputs. Given a set of inputs, the output is assumed to be easily computable and unique. The function is generally assumed to be differentiable. It may contain trainable parameters. Such a module is typically implemented as a deep neural net."
- Non-deterministic functions are represented as a combination of deterministic functions, energy modules, and latent variables, with the latent variable fed to a regularizing energy term. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "Non-deterministic functions do not have a dedicated symbol, but must be represented as a combination of deterministic functions, energy modules, and latent variables."
- The level set Z_h defines the set of latent variables z for which the regularizing energy term R(z) is less than a threshold h. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "We first define Zh as the level set of z, i.e. the set of z for which R(z) is less than a threshold h"
- Energy terms can be transformed into probability distributions using the Gibbs-Boltzmann formula to represent probability distributions over latent variables. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "In some cases, this setup may be used to represent probability distributions. Forst, the energy term is transformed into a probability distribution using a Gibbs-Boltzmann formula"
- Amortized inference reduces the cost of performing latent variable inference by training a neural network encoder to predict approximate solutions to the inference optimization problem. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "One way to reduce the cost of inference is to use amortized inference. The idea is to train a neural net to predict an approximate solution to the inference optimization problem."
- In amortized inference with regularized generative latent-variable EBMs, the regularizer R(z) is critical to prevent the system from cheating by carrying complete information about y through the encoder. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "The regularizer R(z) is even more important than in the regular inference case because the prediction pathway has access to y and can "cheat" by carrying the complete information about y through the encoder."
- Variational Auto-Encoders and LISTA-style sparse Auto-Encoders belong to the family of Regularized GLVEBM with amortized inference, though most are unconditional without x nor Enc(x) modules. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "Variational Auto-Encoders, and LISTA-style sparse Auto-Encoders belong to the family of Regularized GLVEBM with amortized inference."
- Rows 1-2 in the contrastive training table are exact maximum likelihood methods assuming the gradient of the log partition function can be computed exactly, while rows 3-4 are approximate maximum likelihood methods. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "Rows 1-2 in the table are exact maximum likelihood methods. They assume that the gradient of the log partition function can be computed exactly. Rows 2-4 are approximate maximum likelihood methods."
- Maximum Conditional Likelihood for discrete y uses the Gibbs formula to turn energy into a probability distribution and employs negative log conditional likelihood as the loss. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "Row 1: Maximum Conditional Likelihood for discrete y is used whenever the energy needs to be turned into a probability distribution. through a Gibbs formula P (y|x) = exp(−Fw(x, y))/ ∑ y′∈Y exp(−Fw(x, y′). The loss is the negative log conditional likelihood."
- Contrastive Divergence uses MCMC sampling starting from training samples for a short time to generate contrastive samples while satisfying detailed balance. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "One may start from a training sample and let the Markov chain evolve for a short time, and then accept or reject the resulting sample so as to satisfy detailed balance"
- Pairwise hinge loss (triplet loss) drives the energy of the correct output to be lower than contrastive output energy by at least a margin that may grow with divergence between outputs. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "Pairwise hinge, also known as triplet loss, drives the energy of the correct output to be lower than the energy of the contrastive output by at least a margin m(y, ˆy)"
- Min-hinge, Square-hinge, and Square-exp methods assume the energy has a lower bound and minimize energy of correct output while pushing contrastive energies above a margin. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "Assumes that the energy has a lower bound. minimizes the energy of the correct output and pushes the energy of contrastive outputs above a margin equal to m(y, ˆy)"
- GANs differ from other contrastive methods by generating contrastive samples through a generator network trained to preferentially produce samples with low energy according to the model. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "A GAN differs from other contrastive methods in the way contrastive samples are generated. The contrastive samples are produced by a generator network that is trained preferentially generate samples that have low energy according to the model."
- Denoising Auto-Encoders produce contrastive samples by corrupting training outputs through noise or masking, with energy function as the reconstruction error. ([[phd/raw/phd/10356_a_path_towards_autonomous_mach.pdf#pp. 49–62]]) — "A denoising AE produces contrastive samples by corrupting outputs from training samples. The corruption can be performed by adding noise or by masking parts of the output. The energy function is the reconstruction error"
